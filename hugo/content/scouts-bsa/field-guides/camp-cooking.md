Great camp cooking is the engine of high patrol morale. A well-run patrol kitchen operates cleanly, safely, and efficiently, producing nutritious, high-calorie meals while upholding strict sanitation standards and Leave No Trace principles.

## Patrol Kitchen Layout & Food Safety

A campsite kitchen must be organized systematically to prevent foodborne illness, avoid cross-contamination, and protect food from wildlife.

![The Backcountry Bear Triangle](/img/diagrams/bear-triangle.png)

### The 200-Foot Bear Triangle Rule
In bear and critter territory, establish three distinct areas separated by at least **200 feet (70 paces)** in a triangular layout:
1. **Sleeping Area:** Tents must contain zero food, scented toiletries, wrappers, or clothes worn while cooking ("smellables").
2. **Kitchen & Cooking Area:** Downwind from the sleeping area. All cooking, prepping, and dishwashing occurs here.
3. **Food Storage & Gray Water Sump:** Bear-resistant canisters, Ursacks, or counterbalance PCT bear hangs suspended 12 feet high and 6 feet out from tree trunks.

### Food Safety Temperatures & Cooler Management

{{< drg/safety-first >}}
**The Danger Zone (40°F – 140°F / 4°C – 60°C):**
Bacteria double every 20 minutes in this temperature range. Never leave perishable foods (raw meats, dairy, mayonnaise, cooked rice) in the Danger Zone for more than **2 hours** (or 1 hour if ambient temperature exceeds 90°F / 32°C).
{{< /drg/safety-first >}}

| Food Category | Safe Internal Cooking Temp | Target Holding Temp |
| :--- | :--- | :--- |
| **Poultry (Chicken, Turkey)** | **165°F (74°C)** | Hot: > 140°F (60°C) |
| **Ground Meats (Beef, Pork)** | **160°F (71°C)** | Hot: > 140°F (60°C) |
| **Whole Cuts (Steak, Chops, Fish)** | **145°F (63°C)** (with 3-min rest) | Hot: > 140°F (60°C) |
| **Reheated Leftovers / Soups** | **165°F (74°C)** (rolling boil) | Hot: > 140°F (60°C) |
| **Cold Food Storage (Coolers)** | Cold: **< 40°F (4°C)** | Ice submerged |

#### Patrol Cooler Management Rules
- **Block Ice vs. Cubed Ice:** Use frozen solid 1-gallon water jugs or commercial block ice. Block ice lasts 3 to 4 times longer than crushed cubed ice.
- **Pre-Chill Everything:** Chill both the cooler and all food items in a home refrigerator 24 hours prior to packing.
- **Layering Order:** Place block ice on the bottom, raw meat double-sealed in heavy-duty zip bags directly on the ice, dairy and vegetables in the middle, and delicate items (bread, eggs) in elevated dry racks on top.
- **Do Not Drain Meltwater Continuously (Unless Submerging Open Bags):** Cold meltwater insulates the remaining ice better than warm ambient air.

## The BSA 3-Pot Sanitation Dishwashing System

Proper dishwashing in the field prevents debilitating gastrointestinal outbreaks (e.g., *Norovirus*, *Campylobacter*, *E. coli*) that can compromise a trek.

![The BSA 3-Pot Dishwashing Sanitation System](/img/diagrams/three-pot-sanitation.png)

### Step-by-Step Execution

1. **Pre-Scrape ("The Clean Plate Club"):**
   - Each Scout uses a silicone/rubber spatula to scrape every food scrap from their personal mess kit into the trash bag.
   - Mess kits should be wiped nearly clean with a napkin before touching water.
2. **Pot 1 – Hot Wash:**
   - Temperature: **110°F – 120°F** (comfortable to gloved hands).
   - Add a few drops of concentrated biodegradable soap (e.g., CampSuds). Use an abrasive nylon scrub pad to wash plates, cups, and utensils.
3. **Pot 2 – Hot Clear Rinse:**
   - Temperature: **120°F – 140°F** (hotter than wash water).
   - Dip items into clean water to completely rinse away all residual soap film.
4. **Pot 3 – Sanitizing Rinse:**
   - **Method A (Chemical Sanitizer):** Warm water with 1 capful of unscented household bleach per gallon (or 1 commercial chlorine/quaternary sanitizing tablet per gallon). Submerge items for at least **60 seconds**.
   - **Method B (Heat Sanitizer):** Maintain rolling water temperature **> 180°F (82°C)**. Submerge items using metal tongs or a wire dunking basket for at least **30 seconds**.
5. **Air Drying:**
   - Place sanitized dishes into a ventilated mesh dunk bag.
   - Hang the bag from a clothesline in the sun and wind.
   - **Never dry dishes with a dish towel**, which transfers bacteria from plate to plate.

### Sump & Gray Water Disposal

1. Pour the water from Pot 1 and Pot 2 through a fine wire mesh strainer (sump screen) into Pot 3 to catch all suspended food particulates.
2. Dump the trapped food solids into the garbage bag.
3. **Disposal Options:**
   - **Broadcast Method (Leave No Trace standard):** Walk at least **200 feet** away from campsites, trails, and natural water bodies. Swing the bucket in a wide arc to fling the gray water into a fine mist over durable soil, rocks, or brush.
   - **Sump Hole Method (Established high-use camps):** Pour gray water directly into a designated camp sump tube or a 1-foot-deep hole dug in mineral soil 200 feet from water.

## Dutch Oven Mastery & Charcoal Calculator

A standard cast iron Dutch oven is an outdoor convection oven. The flanged lid holds burning charcoal briquettes, allowing top and bottom heat distribution for roasting, simmering, frying, and baking.

![Cast Iron Dutch Oven Anatomy and Heat Distribution](/img/diagrams/dutch-oven-heat-distribution.png)

### Sizing & Capacity Guide

| Oven Size | Liquid Capacity | Feeds (People) | Ideal For |
| :--- | :--- | :--- | :--- |
| **8-Inch** | 2 Quarts | 2 – 4 | Side dishes, hot dips, patrol leader snacks |
| **10-Inch** | 4 Quarts | 4 – 7 | Small patrol cobblers, breakfast casseroles, breads |
| **12-Inch Regular** | 6 Quarts | 8 – 12 | Standard patrol main dishes, stews, 12" cakes |
| **12-Inch Deep** | 8 Quarts | 10 – 14 | Whole chickens, large roasts, deep stews |
| **14-Inch Regular** | 10 Quarts | 14 – 20 | Multi-patrol feasts, large roasts, whole turkeys |

### The Charcoal Rule of Thumb & Cooking Modes

- **The Fundamental Formula:** Total Briquettes = **$2 \times \text{Diameter of the Oven (in inches)}$**
  - *Example:* A 12-inch Dutch oven requires $2 \times 12 = 24$ total briquettes for a standard $350^\circ\text{F}$ bake.

| Cooking Mode | Primary Dishes | Heat Ratio (Top / Bottom) | Rule-of-Thumb Formula | 12-Inch @ 350°F Example |
| :--- | :--- | :--- | :--- | :--- |
| **Baking** | Breads, Cakes, Cobblers, Biscuits | **2/3 on Lid (Top), 1/3 on Bottom** | Top = $D + 3$ \| Bottom = $D - 3$ | **15 on Top, 9 on Bottom** |
| **Roasting** | Whole Chicken, Beef Roast, Pork Loin | **1/2 on Lid (Top), 1/2 on Bottom** | Top = $D$ \| Bottom = $D$ | **12 on Top, 12 on Bottom** |
| **Stewing / Simmering** | Chili, Soups, Gumbo, Beans | **1/3 on Lid (Top), 2/3 on Bottom** | Top = $D - 3$ \| Bottom = $D + 3$ | **9 on Top, 15 on Bottom** |
| **Frying / Boiling** | Pancakes, Bacon, Pasta Water | **100% on Bottom** (no coals on lid) | All coals under oven base | **24 on Bottom** |

{{< drg/tip >}}
**The 15-Minute Dutch Oven Rotation:** To guarantee even browning and prevent hot spots from individual briquettes:
Every 10 to 15 minutes, lift the Dutch oven by its bail handle and rotate the pot **90° clockwise**. Then, use a lid lifter to rotate the lid **90° counter-clockwise**.
{{< /drg/tip >}}

### Weather & Altitude Adjustments
- **High Wind:** Wind strips heat rapidly. Add 2 to 4 briquettes to the top lid and surround the oven with a metal windscreen.
- **Cold Ambient Temp (< 40°F / 4°C):** Add 2 briquettes to the top and 2 to the bottom.
- **High Altitude (> 5,000 ft):** Liquid boils at lower temperatures and heat dissipates faster. Increase cooking time by 10–15% or add 2 briquettes.

### Cast Iron Care & Seasoning Protocol

1. **Daily Cleaning in Camp:**
   - While the oven is still warm, pour in clean hot water.
   - Scrub off residue using a wooden scraper, chainmail scrubber, or coarse kosher salt.
   - **NEVER use liquid dish soap or soak cast iron in water**—soap penetrates the porous iron and strips the polymerized oil seasoning.
2. **Drying & Sanitizing:**
   - Place the cleaned oven over stove heat or hot coals for 2 minutes until all moisture evaporates completely.
3. **Re-Oiling:**
   - While hot, apply a pea-sized drop of high-smoke-point oil (canola, grapeseed, or Crisco) to the interior.
   - Wipe with a clean paper towel until the iron looks matte and dry (never leave pooling liquid oil, which turns rancid).
4. **Storage:**
   - Place a folded paper towel or wooden clothespin between the lid and the pot to allow continuous airflow and prevent moisture condensation.

## Backcountry Backpacking Stoves & Fuel Chemistry

| Stove Category | Fuel Type | Cold Weather Performance | Simmer Control | Pros | Cons |
| :--- | :--- | :--- | :--- | :--- | :--- |
| **Canister (Upright)** | Isobutane / Propane (80/20 mix) | Poor below 25°F (-4°C) | Outstanding | Ultralight (2–3 oz), instant ignition, clean | Non-refillable canisters; poor sub-freezing boil times |
| **Inverted Canister** | Isobutane / Propane | Good down to 10°F (-12°C) | Good | Stable low center of gravity; feeds liquid fuel | Slightly heavier than uprights |
| **Liquid Fuel (White Gas)** | Naphtha (Coleman Fuel) | **Flawless to -40°F (-40°C)** | Moderate to Poor | Extreme high BTU output; works at extreme altitude | Requires manual priming and pumping; heavier |
| **Biomass / Twig Stove** | Dry twigs, pinecones | Dependent on dry fuel | None | Infinite free fuel; ultralight | Banned during wildfire burn bans; soots pots |

{{< drg/did-you-know >}}
The black non-stick patina on well-seasoned cast iron is not grease—it is **polymerized oil**. Under high heat, unsaturated fats chemically cross-link into a natural, rock-hard polymer ceramic bonded directly into the microscopic pores of the iron.
