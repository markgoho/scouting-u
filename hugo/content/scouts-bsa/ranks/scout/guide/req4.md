Rope skills are hands-on — reading about a knot only gets you so far. Have a length of rope with you as you go through this page, and tie along as you read.

## Tie 3 basic knots {#4a}

{{< drg/requirement number="4a" text_format="html" >}}
Show how to tie a square knot, two half-hitches, and a taut-line hitch. Explain how each knot is used.
{{< /drg/requirement >}}

Each of these three knots does a different job, and knowing which one to reach for matters as much as knowing how to tie it.

- **Square knot** — joins two ropes of the same thickness. Used to tie the ends of a bandage, finish off a bundle, or join two rope ends for a short splice. It is not meant for climbing or any load-bearing use — under heavy strain, a square knot can slip.
- **Two half-hitches** — anchors a rope to a fixed object, like a tent stake, a tree, or a pole. Quick to tie and quick to untie, even after it's been under tension.
- **Taut-line hitch** — a sliding knot that holds firm under load but can be adjusted by hand without untying it. This is the knot behind almost every tent guyline, letting you tighten or loosen the line as the wind picks up or the ground shifts overnight.

{{< drg/tip >}}
Learn these by feel, not just by sight. Practice tying each one with your eyes closed once you have the steps down — that's closer to what it's actually like tying a taut-line hitch on a tent in the dark.
{{< /drg/tip >}}

## Whip and fuse rope ends {#4b}

{{< drg/requirement number="4b" text_format="html" >}}
Show the proper care of a rope by learning how to whip and fuse the ends of different kinds of rope.
{{< /drg/requirement >}}

A rope with a frayed, unraveling end is a rope on its way to being useless — and a hazard, since a frayed strand can snag or fail under load without warning. Sealing the end is basic gear maintenance every Scout should know.

**Whipping** works on natural-fiber rope, like manila or cotton, which will melt or burn instead of sealing cleanly. Wrap a short length of strong thread or twine tightly around the rope end several times, then tuck and tie off the ends so the wrapping can't slide loose. This binds the strands together without needing heat.

**Fusing** works on synthetic rope, like nylon or polypropylene. Carefully pass the cut end through a flame just long enough to melt the fibers together into a sealed cap, then let it cool before handling. This only works because synthetic fibers melt instead of burning to ash.

{{< drg/safety-first >}}
Fusing rope means working with an open flame. Do this only with an adult present, keep the flame moving instead of holding it in one spot, and let the melted end cool completely before you touch it — melted nylon holds heat and can cause a serious burn.
{{< /drg/safety-first >}}

Knowing which technique matches which rope material is the real point of this requirement — using the wrong one, like trying to fuse manila rope, won't seal the end at all.
