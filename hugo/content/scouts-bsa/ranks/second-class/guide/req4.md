{{< drg/requirement number="4" text_format="html" >}}
Identify or show evidence of at least 10 kinds of wild animals (such as birds, mammals, reptiles, fish, or mollusks) found in your local area or camping location. You may show evidence by tracks, signs, or photographs you have taken.
{{< /drg/requirement >}}

Most wild animals hear, smell, or see you long before you spot them. In the outdoors, true wildlife identification relies as much on discovering and interpreting the evidence animals leave behind—tracks, feeding signs, scat, vocalizations, and shelters—as on direct visual sightings.

## The Scope: What Counts as an Animal

The requirement encompasses both **vertebrates** (animals with a backbone) and **invertebrates** (animals without a backbone). To fulfill the requirement, you must identify or show evidence of at least **10 distinct wild animal species** found in your local community or camping areas. You should aim for a diverse mix across the major taxonomic groups:

1. **Mammals**: Warm-blooded vertebrates covered with fur or hair that nurse their young with milk (e.g., white-tailed deer, raccoon, eastern gray squirrel, coyote, striped skunk, beaver, opossum).
2. **Birds**: Warm-blooded vertebrates with feathers, beaked jaws, wings, and hard-shelled eggs (e.g., red-tailed hawk, great blue heron, mallard, northern cardinal, eastern bluebird, wild turkey).
3. **Reptiles**: Cold-blooded, air-breathing vertebrates with dry, scaly skin or protective shells (e.g., eastern box turtle, painted turtle, garter snake, black rat snake, green anole).
4. **Amphibians**: Cold-blooded vertebrates that typically begin life in water with gills and develop lungs as land-dwelling adults, characterized by moist, permeable skin (e.g., American bullfrog, spring peeper, spotted salamander, American toad).
5. **Fish**: Cold-blooded aquatic vertebrates that breathe through gills and maneuver with fins (e.g., largemouth bass, bluegill sunfish, brook trout, channel catfish).
6. **Mollusks & Arthropods**: Invertebrates including shelled mollusks (freshwater mussels, land snails) and arthropods with jointed exoskeletons (dragonflies, butterflies, crayfish, orb-weaver spiders).

## Gathering Evidence: The Four Senses

{{< drg/image src="images/common-animal-tracks.png" alt="Side-by-side comparison of common mammal track patterns, sized and labeled by species" >}}

When searching for wildlife in your community or on the trail, employ four observational senses:

### 1. Looking (Visual Evidence)
- **Direct Sighting**: Observing the animal itself. Look along forest edges, tree canopies, pond margins, and brush piles. Use binoculars to scan distant ridges and shorelines.
- **Tracks & Footprints**: Imprints left in soft mud, wet sand, snow, or dusty trail beds.
- **Feeding Signs**: Gnawed pinecones (squirrels), stripped tree bark or felled saplings (beavers), clipped twigs with a clean 45-degree angle cut (rabbits) or torn ragged edges (deer), cracked nutshells, or woodpecker drilling holes in dead snags.
- **Rubbing & Scratch Marks**: Antler rubs where male deer strip bark from saplings to remove velvet and mark territory; claw scratches on tree trunks (black bears or bobcats).
- **Shelters & Homes**: Nests, hollow-tree cavities, underground burrows, beaver lodges, or spider webs.
- **Remains**: Shed snake skins, molted feathers, tufts of hair snagged on barbed wire or thorny briars, or owl pellets (compact regurgitated masses containing rodent skulls and bones).

### 2. Listening (Auditory Evidence)
- **Vocalizations & Songs**: Identifying bird species by distinct calls (such as the rhythmic "who-cooks-for-you" of a barred owl or the harsh cawing of an American crow).
- **Mating & Territorial Choruses**: Spring choruses of tree frogs, spring peepers, and bullfrogs near standing water.
- **Alarm & Warning Signals**: The chattering bark of a gray squirrel alerting the forest to a predator, the loud slap of a beaver's tail against the water surface, or the sudden alarm shrieks of blue jays.
- **Physical Movement**: Rustling in dry forest leaves, crashing brush, or the splashing of fish surfacing at dawn and dusk.

### 3. Smelling (Olfactory Evidence)
- Scent marking is a primary communication tool in the animal kingdom. Notice the distinct pungent sulfurous odor of a striped skunk, the musky aroma of a fox den, or the strong territorial scent marks of deer scrapes and beaver castoreum mounds along waterways.

### 4. Touching & Examining
- Touch and examine textures of fallen feathers, shed skins, and animal bedding depressions in tall grass.
- **Scat (Dropping) Analysis**: Examining animal droppings provides vital clues about an animal's identity and diet. **Safety rule: Never touch scat with your bare hands; use a clean stick to inspect it.**
  - *Herbivore Scat*: Composed entirely of digested plant fibers, seeds, and wood pulp. Deer and rabbits produce distinct round, pellet-like droppings.
  - *Carnivore Scat*: Tapered, cylindrical droppings containing fur, feathers, and small bone fragments (e.g., foxes, bobcats, owls).
  - *Omnivore Scat*: Irregular droppings containing a mix of berry seeds, insect exoskeletons, plant matter, and animal hair (e.g., raccoons, coyotes, black bears).

## Reading and Preserving Animal Tracks

Tracks reveal not just an animal's species, but its speed, direction, weight, and behavior.

### Key Track Distinctions
- **Canine vs. Feline**:
  - *Canine (Dog, Fox, Coyote, Wolf)*: 4 toe pads with prominent, non-retractable claw marks visible in front of each toe. An **"X" pattern** of negative space can be drawn cleanly between the heel pad and the toe pads without crossing them.
  - *Feline (House Cat, Bobcat, Cougar)*: 4 tear-drop shaped toe pads with **no claw marks** (claws remain retracted while walking). The heel pad has three distinct rear lobes and a single depression in front, creating a curved "C" shape of negative space.
- **Rodents & Lagomorphs (Rabbits / Hares)**: When bounding, rabbits and squirrels place their small front paws down first, then swing their larger hind feet ahead to land *in front of* the forepaws.
- **Raccoons & Opossums**: Both have 5 elongated, highly dexterous toes on each foot that resemble small human hands.

### Making a Plaster Track Cast
Preserving a clean track creates a permanent record for your wildlife log:
1. **Find a Clear Print**: Locate a sharp, deep footprint in firm mud or damp sand. Carefully remove loose twigs or leaves from inside the print.
2. **Set a Collar**: Cut a strip of thin cardboard or plastic about 2 inches wide. Form it into a ring or oval around the print, overlapping the ends and securing them with a paperclip or tape. Press the collar 1/2 inch into the ground around the track.
3. **Mix the Plaster**: In a clean disposable container, add Plaster of Paris to clean water, stirring until it reaches the consistency of smooth, thick pancake batter (no lumps).
4. **Pour the Cast**: Pour the plaster mixture gently inside the cardboard ring, letting it flow into the depressions of the track without splashing or dislodging fine soil details. Fill the collar to a depth of about 1 inch.
5. **Cure and Finish**: Allow the cast to harden undisturbed for **15 to 20 minutes**. Once firm, gently dig beneath the collar and lift the cast out. Allow it to fully dry for several hours, then brush away remaining soil under running water. On the back, use a waterproof marker to record the date, location, habitat type, and animal species.

## Outdoor Ethics and Safe Stalking

When searching for wildlife, always prioritize animal welfare and safety:
- **Keep Your Distance**: Never crowd, chase, or corner a wild animal. Use binoculars, zoom lenses, or spotting scopes to observe natural behaviors without causing stress.
- **Respect Nests, Dens, and Young**: Never touch or disturb animal nests, eggs, burrows, or newborn animals. A mother is often foraging nearby; human scent can cause parents to abandon their offspring or attract predators.
- **Stalking Techniques**: Move slowly and silently along forest edges. Step heel-to-toe to avoid snapping dry twigs. Keep your silhouette low against ridges, and always travel **upwind** (facing into the breeze) so your human scent does not blow toward the animals.
- **Never Feed Wildlife**: Feeding wild animals damages their digestive systems, makes them dependent on humans, and creates dangerous nuisance behavior.
