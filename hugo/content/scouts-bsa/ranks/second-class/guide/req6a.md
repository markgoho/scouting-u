{{< drg/requirement number="6a" text_format="html" >}}
Demonstrate first aid for the following:
{{< /drg/requirement >}}

- Object in the eye
- Bite of a suspected rabid animal
- Puncture wounds from a splinter, nail, and fishhook
- Serious burns (partial thickness, or second-degree)
- Heat exhaustion
- Shock
- Heatstroke, dehydration, hypothermia, and hyperventilation

At Tenderfoot you [treated cuts, scrapes, blisters, and minor burns](/scouts-bsa/ranks/tenderfoot/guide/req4a/). Second Class demonstrates first aid for seven categories that go further — from something as ordinary as an eyelash in your eye to a heat emergency that can turn dangerous fast.

## Object in the Eye

Don't rub the eye — that can drive debris deeper or scratch the surface. Try flushing gently with clean water, or lift the upper lid over the lower lid to let tears wash the object out. If it doesn't clear or is embedded, cover both eyes loosely and get medical help.

## Bite of a Suspected Rabid Animal

Wash the wound thoroughly with soap and water for several minutes, control any bleeding, and cover it. This bite needs medical attention regardless of how minor it looks — rabies is fatal once symptoms start, so identify the animal if it's safe to do so and report the bite promptly.

## Puncture Wounds: Splinter, Nail, Fishhook

For a **splinter**, remove it with clean tweezers along the angle it entered, then wash and cover. For a **nail**, control bleeding and seek medical care — deep puncture wounds carry a real infection risk. For a **fishhook**, if the barb hasn't gone all the way through, don't force it back out; get medical help to remove it safely.

## Serious Burns (Partial Thickness / Second-Degree)

Cool the burn under cool (not ice-cold) running water for several minutes, cover loosely with a clean, non-stick dressing, and get medical care — second-degree burns blister and damage more than the surface layer of skin. Never pop a burn blister or apply butter or ointment.

## Heat Exhaustion

Watch for heavy sweating, weakness, cool clammy skin, and nausea. Move the person to shade, loosen clothing, cool them with water, and have them sip water slowly. Heat exhaustion can progress to heatstroke if it isn't treated.

## Shock

Signs include pale or clammy skin, rapid weak pulse, and confusion. Lay the person down, keep them warm, and treat any underlying injury — shock often accompanies another emergency, so keep watching for the cause.

## Heatstroke, Dehydration, Hypothermia, and Hyperventilation

- **Heatstroke**: hot, dry or flushed skin and confusion — a life-threatening emergency. Cool the person immediately by any means available and get emergency help.
- **Dehydration**: thirst, dry mouth, and dark urine. Rehydrate gradually with water.
- **Hypothermia**: shivering, confusion, and slurred speech. Get the person dry, insulated, and warmed gradually — never rub cold skin briskly.
- **Hyperventilation**: rapid breathing and lightheadedness. Help the person calm down and slow their breathing.

{{< drg/safety-first >}}
Heatstroke and severe hypothermia are medical emergencies. Cool or warm the person while someone else calls for help — don't wait to see if it resolves on its own.
