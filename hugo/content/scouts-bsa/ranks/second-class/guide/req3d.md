{{< drg/requirement number="3d" text_format="html" >}}
Demonstrate how to find directions during the day and at night without using a compass or an electronic device.
{{< /drg/requirement >}}

A dead phone battery or a compass left behind shouldn't mean you're lost — this requirement is your backup plan when the tools from requirement 3a aren't available.

## Finding Direction During the Day

The most reliable daytime method uses a stick and its shadow:

1. Push a straight stick into level ground so it casts a clear shadow.
2. Mark the tip of the shadow.
3. Wait 15-20 minutes, then mark the new shadow tip.
4. Draw a line between the two marks — this line runs roughly east to west, with the first mark toward the west.

The sun itself is also a rough guide: it rises generally in the east and sets generally in the west, which is enough to keep you oriented in a pinch even without the shadow-stick method.

## Finding Direction at Night

In the Northern Hemisphere, Polaris — the North Star — sits almost exactly above true north and doesn't move as the rest of the sky rotates around it. Find it using the Big Dipper: follow the two stars at the outer edge of the Dipper's "cup" in a straight line, about five times the distance between them, and you'll land on Polaris.

{{< drg/image src="images/polaris-big-dipper.png" alt="Night sky diagram showing the Big Dipper's pointer stars leading to Polaris" >}}

{{< drg/did-you-know >}}
Polaris isn't the brightest star in the sky — it just happens to sit almost exactly on Earth's axis of rotation, which is why it appears to stay still while everything else circles around it.
{{< /drg/did-you-know >}}

{{< drg/tip >}}
Practice the shadow-stick method on a sunny afternoon before you need it for real. Knowing the steps in theory is very different from having actually watched a shadow move.
{{< /drg/tip >}}
