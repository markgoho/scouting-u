{{< drg/requirement number="3c" text_format="html" >}}
Describe some hazards or injuries that you might encounter on your hike and what you can do to help prevent them.
{{< /drg/requirement >}}

Anticipating potential trail hazards before you leave the trailhead allows you to pack the right equipment, choose safe routes, and prevent minor discomforts from turning into medical emergencies five miles into the backcountry.

## Common Trail Hazards, Prevention, and Response

### 1. Blisters and Foot Hot Spots
- **Cause**: Friction between your sock and skin combined with heat and moisture.
- **Prevention**: Break in hiking boots thoroughly before the trek; wear clean, well-fitting synthetic or merino wool socks (or a thin liner sock beneath wool hiking socks); keep feet dry.
- **Response**: The instant you feel a "hot spot" (burning or rubbing sensation), stop immediately! Do not wait for a full blister. Apply a piece of **moleskin**, leukotape, or athletic tape directly over the reddened area. If a blister has formed, cut a doughnut-shaped ring of moleskin around it to relieve pressure.

### 2. Dehydration and Heat Exhaustion
- **Cause**: Losing water and electrolytes through heavy sweating in hot or humid weather.
- **Prevention**: Drink water regularly (sip every 15 to 20 minutes); wear lightweight, light-colored clothing and a wide-brimmed hat; take rest breaks in shaded areas.
- **Response**: Move the person to shade, loosen tight clothing, fan them gently, and give small sips of cool water or an electrolyte drink.

### 3. Hypothermia and Sudden Chilling
- **Cause**: Exposure to cold air, rain, wind, or perspiration dampness ("cotton is rotten").
- **Prevention**: Dress in layers (synthetic base layer, fleece/wool insulating mid-layer, waterproof breathable rain shell); put on rain gear before you get wet; change out of sweaty clothes during long stops.
- **Response**: Shelter from wind and rain, replace wet clothing with dry layers, wrap in blankets or a sleeping bag, and give warm, sweet liquids if the person is conscious and alert.

### 4. Poisonous Plants (Poison Ivy, Oak, Sumac)
- **Cause**: Contact with urushiol oil found on the leaves, stems, and roots of poisonous plants.
- **Prevention**: Learn local plant identification ("Leaves of three, let it be"); stay in the center of trails; wear long hiking pants and high socks.
- **Response**: Wash skin within 10 to 15 minutes with cold water and degreasing soap (or Tecnu); avoid scratching; apply calamine lotion to soothe itching.

### 5. Ticks and Biting Insects
- **Cause**: Ticks resting on tall grass and low brush waiting to attach to warm-blooded hosts.
- **Prevention**: Treat boots and clothing with permethrin; apply DEET or Picaridin repellent to exposed skin; tuck pant legs into socks; perform regular buddy tick checks during rest breaks.
- **Response**: Remove attached ticks immediately using fine-tipped tweezers, grasping the tick as close to the skin as possible and pulling straight out with steady pressure. Disinfect the bite area.

### 6. Terrain and Trail Hazards
- **Cause**: Loose scree, exposed roots, slippery wet boulders, and steep drop-offs.
- **Prevention**: Step over logs and rocks rather than on them; maintain steady footing; slow down on descents; unbuckle backpack hip belts during deep stream crossings so you can shed the pack quickly if you slip.

{{< drg/safety-first >}}
If you hear thunder or see towering cumulonimbus clouds developing, get off high mountain ridges, exposed peaks, and open fields immediately. Descend into a low area with uniform tree cover and avoid isolated tall trees or shallow rock overhangs.
{{< /drg/safety-first >}}

In your Scoutmaster conference, be prepared to discuss the specific hazards relevant to your troop's local terrain (e.g., desert heat, rocky mountain switchbacks, or marshy coastal humidity).
{{< /drg/tip >}}
