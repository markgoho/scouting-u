{{< drg/requirement number="5b" text_format="html" >}}
Describe what to do if you become lost on a hike or campout.
{{< /drg/requirement >}}

Even careful hikers get turned around. What separates a brief scare from a real emergency is what you do in the first few minutes after you realize you're lost.

{{< drg/be-prepared title="You Realize You've Wandered Off the Trail" >}}
Panicking and moving fast in a random direction is how a lost hiker becomes a hard-to-find hiker.

- **Step one**: Stop moving. Every step in the wrong direction makes you harder to find.
- **Step two**: Stay calm and think back to your last known point on the trail or map.
- **Step three**: Make yourself easy to find — stay in an open area if possible, and use a whistle (three blasts is the universal distress signal) or shout at intervals.
- **Step four**: Hug a tree — stay put once you know you're lost, unless you have a clear, confirmed way back. Searchers can't find someone who keeps moving.
{{< /drg/be-prepared >}}

## Why Staying Put Works

A search party plans around your last known location and how far someone could realistically travel from it. Every hour you keep moving expands that search area — sometimes past what searchers can reasonably cover before dark. Staying in place keeps you inside a search radius that rescuers can actually work through.

{{< drg/safety-first >}}
Carry a whistle on every hike — it carries farther and lasts longer than your voice, and three blasts of any kind (whistle, horn, or shout) is recognized worldwide as a distress signal.
{{< /drg/safety-first >}}
