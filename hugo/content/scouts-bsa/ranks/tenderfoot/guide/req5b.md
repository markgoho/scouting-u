{{< drg/requirement number="5b" text_format="html" >}}
Describe what to do if you become lost on a hike or campout.
{{< /drg/requirement >}}

Even experienced outdoors enthusiasts can occasionally take a wrong turn. What separates a minor inconvenience from a dangerous survival situation is how you react the moment you realize you are lost.

The most important rule when lost is to **stay calm and stay put**. Panicking, running, or wandering aimlessly in hopes of finding the trail almost always leads you deeper into unfamiliar terrain and makes search efforts exponentially harder.

## The STOP Method

The moment you realize you do not know where you are or cannot see your patrol, follow the **STOP** steps:

{{< drg/be-prepared title="The STOP Protocol" >}}
- **S — Stop (and Sit Down)**: Freeze in your tracks. Sit down on a log or rock, take a deep breath, and take a sip of water. Acknowledge that you are lost without panicking. Calming your body stops the rush of adrenaline that causes reckless decisions.
- **T — Think**: How did you get here? When did you last see a familiar trail blaze, landmark, bridge, or member of your group? Can you see the trail within a few paces? If not, do not guess or wander—stay where you are.
- **O — Observe**: Look around in all directions. Notice terrain features: ridges, streams, clearings, or distinct rock formations. Listen carefully for distant voices, vehicle traffic, running water, or whistles. Check your gear, water supply, and remaining daylight.
- **P — Plan**: Make a calm, practical plan based on your situation. If you do not have a 100% certain, visible route back to the trail, your plan is simple: **Stay Put ("Hug a Tree")**, make yourself visible, and prepare for searchers to arrive.
{{< /drg/be-prepared >}}

## Why Staying Put ("Hug a Tree") Saves Lives

When leaders or search-and-rescue teams realize a Scout is missing, they start their search from your **last known point**. They search in expanding circles outward from that spot:

- If you **stay put**, searchers will find you quickly because you remain within their initial search area.
- If you **keep walking**, you expand the search area by miles, potentially walking faster than searchers can sweep, and you risk walking into dangerous terrain or cliffs.

## Signaling for Help: The Rule of Threes

In the outdoors, **three of any signal** is the universal distress call:

- **Whistle (Best Tool)**: Blow **three sharp, loud blasts** on your whistle. Pause for one to two minutes to listen for a response, then repeat. A whistle's high-pitch sound carries much farther than human shouts and does not exhaust your vocal cords.
- **Visual Signals**:
  - During the day: Spread out bright clothing or emergency foil blankets in an open clearing. If available, use a signal mirror to flash toward search planes or distant ridges.
  - At night: Flash a flashlight or headlamp in sets of three flashes.
- **Audio Shouts**: If you lack a whistle, shout "HELP!" three times in rapid succession, then listen.

## Preparing to Wait

If evening approaches and you have not been reached:

1. **Find shelter from wind and wet**: Pick a dry, level spot protected from wind (avoid cold canyon bottoms or exposed hilltops).
2. **Insulate from the cold ground**: Sit on your backpack, a sleeping pad, or a thick pile of dry leaves and pine boughs. The ground saps body heat much faster than the air.
3. **Put on warm layers**: Put on rain gear and warm layers from your Scout Basic Essentials before you get cold or damp.

{{< drg/safety-first >}}
Never leave a trail to take a "shortcut" through dense brush or down a steep ravine. Shortcuts are the single most common reason hikers get lost.
{{< /drg/safety-first >}}

{{< drg/tip >}}
Always keep your whistle attached to your backpack strap or around your neck on every hike so it is instantly accessible without opening your pack.
{{< /drg/tip >}}
