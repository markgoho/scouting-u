{{< drg/requirement number="5d" text_format="html" >}}
Explain why it is important to hike on trails or other durable surfaces, and give examples of durable surfaces you saw on your outing.
{{< /drg/requirement >}}

One hiker stepping off-trail leaves barely a mark. A thousand hikers doing the same thing, in the same spot, over a season — that turns a patch of undergrowth into a bare, eroded scar that takes years to recover, if it ever does.

## Why Durable Surfaces Matter

A durable surface is one that can absorb foot traffic without being damaged: rock, gravel, sand, dry grass, established trail tread, or snow. Fragile surfaces — wet meadows, cryptobiotic soil crust in deserts, streambank vegetation — get damaged easily and recover slowly, sometimes over decades. Sticking to durable surfaces spreads impact where the land can handle it and keeps the rest undisturbed.

## Spotting Durable Surfaces

On your own outing, look for examples like:

- A hard-packed dirt or gravel trail tread.
- Bare rock or slickrock.
- A previously established campsite rather than fresh ground.

Compare that to fragile ground you deliberately avoided — soft meadow grass, streamside plants, moss — and be ready to describe both from your actual hike, not from a textbook example.

{{< drg/tip >}}
When a trail braids into multiple parallel paths around a muddy spot, stay on the main tread even if it means getting your boots wet. Walking around the mud is what creates the extra paths in the first place.
{{< /drg/tip >}}
