{{< drg/requirement number="4d" text_format="html" >}}
Assemble a personal first-aid kit to carry with you on future campouts and hikes. Tell how each item in the kit would be used.
{{< /drg/requirement >}}

A first-aid kit only helps if it matches the injuries you're actually likely to face — which is exactly what requirements 4a and 4b just covered. A personal kit handles scrapes, blisters, and other minor injuries; a larger patrol or home kit is what you lean on for more serious emergencies. Building your own kit, instead of grabbing a generic one off a shelf, is what makes this requirement stick.

The requirement doesn't tell you exactly what goes in the kit. The checklist below is the one suggested in the Scouts BSA Handbook. It's a solid starting point, but it isn't a list you have to match item for item. You can add, swap, or leave out items to fit your trips and your own needs. What the requirement does ask is that you can explain how every item in *your* kit would be used.

{{< drg/checklist title="Personal First-Aid Kit" subtitle="Suggested by the Scouts BSA Handbook — not a required list" >}}

- 6 latex-free adhesive bandages, assorted sizes: for small cuts and scrapes.
- 2 sterile 3-by-3-inch gauze pads: to cover larger wounds or apply pressure to stop bleeding.
- A small roll of latex-free adhesive tape: to hold gauze in place, or to tape over a hot spot.
- A 3-by-6-inch piece of moleskin: cut to size to pad hot spots and blisters.
- A small bar of soap or small bottle of alcohol-based hand-sanitizing gel: to clean your hands and the wound area before treating.
- A small tube of triple antibiotic ointment (optional): to help keep a cleaned wound from getting infected.
- A small tube of hydrocortisone cream (optional): to calm itching from insect bites and plant rashes.
- Scissors: to cut tape, gauze, and moleskin.
- Tweezers: for splinters and ticks.
- Disposable latex-free gloves: to protect both you and the person you're treating.
- CPR breathing barrier: to give rescue breaths safely if someone stops breathing.
- Pencil and paper or a small notebook: to record what happened, the time, and the treatment given — useful for adult leaders or emergency responders.
{{< /drg/checklist >}}

{{< drg/safety-first >}}
Some people are allergic to antibiotic ointments and hydrocortisone cream. Always ask permission before applying either one to someone else.
{{< /drg/safety-first >}}

## Tell How You'd Use Each Item

The requirement doesn't just ask you to pack a kit — it asks you to explain each item's purpose. Here's what each item in the suggested handbook kit is for, and which injuries from requirement 4a it helps with. If your kit includes something different, be ready to explain that item the same way. Read through it, then practice explaining your own kit out loud, holding up each item as you go.

- **Adhesive bandages (6, assorted sizes):** Cover small cuts and scrapes after you've cleaned them, so dirt stays out while the skin heals. Small sizes fit fingers and knuckles; larger ones cover scraped knees and elbows. A bandage also works over a popped blister once it's been washed.
- **Sterile 3-by-3-inch gauze pads (2):** For wounds too big for an adhesive bandage. Press a pad firmly on a bleeding cut to stop the bleeding, then tape it in place as a dressing. Gauze also makes a loose, clean cover for a minor burn after it's been cooled with water. "Sterile" means it's germ-free until you open the wrapper — so don't open it until you need it.
- **Latex-free adhesive tape:** Holds gauze pads in place. It's also a quick fix for a hot spot on your foot — tape over the rubbing spot before it turns into a blister. Latex-free matters because some people have latex allergies.
- **Moleskin (3-by-6-inch piece):** A soft, sticky-backed felt pad for hot spots and blisters. Cut a piece a little larger than the blister, cut a doughnut hole in the middle, and stick it on so the pad takes the rubbing instead of the blister.
- **Soap or alcohol-based hand-sanitizing gel:** Clean your hands before and after giving first aid, so germs from your hands don't get into the wound. Soap and clean water are also the best way to wash out a cut, scrape, tick bite, or popped blister.
- **Triple antibiotic ointment (optional):** A thin layer on a cleaned cut or scrape helps prevent infection. Don't put it on burns, and ask permission first — some people are allergic.
- **Hydrocortisone cream (optional):** Calms the itching and swelling from insect bites and stings, and from rashes caused by plants like poison ivy (requirement 4b). Ask permission first here too.
- **Scissors:** Cut tape, gauze, and moleskin to the right size. In an emergency, they can also cut away clothing around an injury.
- **Tweezers:** Pull out splinters, and remove ticks by grasping the tick close to the skin and pulling straight out with steady pressure. You can also use them to pick small bits of dirt or gravel out of a scrape.
- **Disposable latex-free gloves:** Put these on before touching anyone else's blood or wound. They protect you from germs in the other person's blood, and protect the wound from germs on your hands.
- **CPR breathing barrier:** A thin shield you place over a person's mouth if you need to give rescue breaths during CPR — for example, if someone who was choking becomes unconscious and stops breathing. It keeps you from direct mouth-to-mouth contact.
- **Pencil and paper or small notebook:** Write down what happened, when it happened, what you saw, and what first aid you gave. If you need to send for help, write the location and the person's condition so the messenger gets it right. Adult leaders, parents, and emergency responders will want this information.

{{< drg/tip >}}
Notice that some injuries from 4a don't need anything from your kit. A nosebleed just needs you to lean forward and pinch. Frostbite needs gentle warmth. Choking needs abdominal thrusts. Knowing when your hands and your training are the real tools is part of explaining your kit well.
{{< /drg/tip >}}

{{< drg/tip >}}
Pack everything in a resealable plastic bag so it stays dry, and check it before every trip — replace anything you've used and swap out anything that's expired. Add any personal medication you need, clearly labeled.
{{< /drg/tip >}}

Size your kit to fit in your pack without adding much weight. A first-aid kit that's too bulky to carry ends up left at home — which defeats the purpose.

If you have a smartphone, ask your parent or guardian about downloading the American Red Cross's free First Aid app. It includes videos, step-by-step instructions for common injuries, and quizzes to test your knowledge.
