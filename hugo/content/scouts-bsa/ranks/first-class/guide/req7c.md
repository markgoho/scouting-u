{{< drg/requirement number="7c" text_format="html" >}}
Tell the five most common signals of a heart attack. Explain the steps (procedures) in cardiopulmonary resuscitation (CPR).
{{< /drg/requirement >}}

Recognizing a heart attack fast, and knowing what to do if a heart stops, are two separate skills this requirement asks you to explain — not perform on a person, since CPR always requires hands-on certified training to actually use.

## Tell the Five Most Common Signals of a Heart Attack

- Chest pain, pressure, or squeezing that lasts more than a few minutes, or that goes away and comes back.
- Pain spreading to the shoulder, arm, back, neck, or jaw.
- Shortness of breath, with or without chest discomfort.
- Cold sweat, nausea, or lightheadedness.
- A sense that something is seriously wrong, sometimes with no pain at all — especially in women, who more often have subtler symptoms.

{{< drg/safety-first >}}
If you or someone with you shows these signs, call 911 immediately. Don't wait to see if it passes, and don't try to drive the person to a hospital yourself — emergency responders can begin treatment the moment they arrive.
{{< /drg/safety-first >}}

## Explain the Steps of CPR

CPR keeps blood moving to the brain and organs when the heart has stopped, buying time until emergency responders or an AED (automated external defibrillator) can restart it.

1. Check the scene for safety, then check if the person responds. If not, call 911 (or have someone else call) and get an AED if one is available.
2. Position the person on their back on a firm surface.
3. Give chest compressions: push hard and fast in the center of the chest, about two inches deep, at a rate of 100 to 120 compressions per minute.
4. If trained, give two rescue breaths after every 30 compressions. If untrained or unwilling to give breaths, continuous compressions alone still help.
5. Continue cycles of compressions (and breaths) until the person responds, an AED is ready to use, or emergency responders take over.

Explaining these steps satisfies this requirement. Actually performing CPR on a real person requires certified hands-on training from an organization like the American Red Cross or American Heart Association — consider taking a course as a next step beyond what's required here.
