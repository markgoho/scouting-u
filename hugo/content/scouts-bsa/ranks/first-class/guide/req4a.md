{{< drg/requirement number="4a" text_format="html" >}}
Using a map and compass, complete an orienteering course that covers at least one mile and requires measuring the height and/or width of designated items (tree, tower, canyon, ditch, etc.).
{{< /drg/requirement >}}

A canyon is too wide to stretch a tape measure across, and a tower is too tall to climb with one. Surveyors and foresters solve this problem every day without ever touching the thing they're measuring — and this requirement asks you to do the same.

You oriented a map with a compass for Second Class 3a. This requirement builds on that skill directly: you'll use your compass to follow bearings between points on a real course, and you'll cover real ground doing it — at least one mile, start to finish.

## Setting Up the Course

Ask your Scoutmaster or a merit badge counselor with orienteering experience about a course near you — many councils maintain a permanent one, or your troop can lay out a short course on a hike. The course needs a series of numbered points (usually marked with flags or posts) connected by compass bearings and distances. You'll navigate from point to point using only your map and compass, confirming each point as you reach it.

## Measuring What You Can't Reach

At some point on the course, you'll measure the height or width of something — a tree, a tower, a canyon, a ditch. You don't need a tape measure that spans the whole distance. You need geometry.

**Measuring height (the stick method):** Hold a stick vertically at arm's length. Walk backward from the object until the top of the stick, as you see it, lines up with the top of the object and the bottom of the stick lines up with its base. The distance from your feet to the base of the object is a close estimate of the object's height, if your arm length and the stick's length above your fist are roughly equal — this is the same principle as similar triangles.

{{< drg/image src="images/height-measurement-stick-method.png" alt="A Scout sighting a tree's height using the stick method, with sightlines from the eye to the stick's top and the tree's top" >}}

**Measuring width (the baseline method):** Pick a point directly across from a landmark on the far side (a tree, a rock). Walk a measured baseline at a right angle to your sightline — say, 60 feet — and place a stake. From that stake, sight a line to a second reference point that, combined with your original position and the landmark, forms a right triangle you can solve. Simpler in the field: pace off a baseline, then use your compass to sight the angle to the landmark from both ends and apply basic trigonometry, or estimate proportionally if your troop hasn't covered the math yet.

{{< drg/image src="images/width-measurement-baseline-method.png" alt="Overhead diagram of the baseline-and-sighting right triangle used to estimate a landmark's width across a gap" >}}

{{< drg/tip >}}
Practice the height and width methods in your backyard or a local park before you're on the actual course — trying geometry for the first time while also navigating between orienteering points is more than you need to juggle at once.
{{< /drg/tip >}}

{{< drg/did-you-know >}}
Orienteering started as military training in Sweden in the late 1800s. Today it's a competitive sport with its own World Championships, where elite athletes read a map and run a course simultaneously — at a dead sprint.
{{< /drg/did-you-know >}}

{{< drg/checklist title="Before You Start the Course" subtitle="Confirm you're ready" >}}
- Compass: know how to take and follow a bearing.
- Map: know how to orient it to true or magnetic north.
- Measuring tool: a stick, cord, or pace count you've practiced with.
- Distance: confirm the course covers at least one mile.
